The Idea

Imagine you want to tell someone what you need, but in a really simple way. That's what a User Story is.

It always follows the same shape, like a sentence template:

As a [someone], I want [something], so that [why I want it].

Think of it like ordering food.

As a hungry kid, I want a sandwich, so that I'm not hungry anymore.

That's it. Three parts. Who wants it, what they want, and why they want it.

Why Write It This Way

If you just say "I want a sandwich," people don't know who you are or why you want it. Maybe you're allergic to something. Maybe you're in a hurry. Maybe you just had lunch and don't need one.

By saying who you are and why, everyone understands your real need, not just what you asked for on the surface.

That's exactly what happens at work. Instead of a sandwich, it might be a button on an app, a report, or a new feature. Same idea, same three parts.

The INVEST Test

Once you write a User Story, you check if it's a good one using a simple test called INVEST. Each letter checks one thing.

IIndependent

Can this story stand on its own, without needing another story to happen first? Like a sandwich order that doesn't depend on someone else's order first.

NNegotiable

Does it describe the need, not force one exact solution? "I want a sandwich" is negotiable. "I want exactly two slices of white bread, cut diagonally" is too rigid.

VValuable

Does it actually help someone? A sandwich helps a hungry kid. A story with no real reason behind it isn't valuable.

EEstimable

Can someone guess how much time or effort it takes? "Make a sandwich" is easy to estimate. "Make the best meal in the world" is not.

SSmall

Is it one clear task, not a giant list of tasks? "Make a sandwich" is small. "Cook a five course dinner for 50 people" is way too big.

TTestable

Can you check if it's done or not? You can look at the sandwich and know: yes, it's made, or no, it's not.

Three Examples, Simple to Advanced

EXAMPLE 1

Scenario: A student wants to check their grades without going to the school office.

As a student, I want to see my grades online, so that I don't need to visit the school office.

The person is clear (student), the action is clear (see grades online), the reason is clear (avoid an office visit). It's small, testable, and clearly valuable.

EXAMPLE 2

Scenario: A store has noticed that many customers add items to their online cart but never buy them. The marketing team wants to understand this pattern.

As a marketing team member, I want a list of products frequently added to cart but not purchased, so that I can create promotions to encourage those purchases.

The story stays negotiable, since it doesn't dictate the report design. It's still small, one clear deliverable, not a whole analytics platform, with a real reason behind it.

EXAMPLE 3

Scenario: A hospital emergency room has dozens of patients on the list at once. Nurses need to find critical patients quickly, but currently scroll through everyone, wasting time when every second matters.

As an ER nurse, I want to filter the patient list to show only critical cases, so that I can respond faster without manually searching through everyone.

The persona had to be picked carefully, an ER nurse specifically, not "hospital staff" in general. The value goes beyond convenience, it's about response speed in a life or death setting.
